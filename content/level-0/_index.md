{{< lead >}}
The Greek alphabet is the single largest barrier a beginner meets, and it is
not a barrier that disappears by being ignored. Level 0 treats reading
readiness as a learning objective in its own right: six units that take a
learner from *not recognising a letter* to *decoding any Greek word aloud and
typing it with the correct accent*.
{{< /lead >}}
{{< /hero >}}

## Why a level before A1

Courses in Latin-script languages can put a greeting on page one, because the
learner can already read it. Greek cannot. A course that prints *γεια σου* on
the first page and hopes the alphabet will be absorbed on the way produces
learners who guess at words, mispronounce the digraphs, and quietly fall back
on transliteration for months.

Level 0 therefore does the work explicitly and finishes it. It is short —
six units, roughly two to three weeks at school pace — and it is a real
prerequisite: A1 Unit 1 assumes every letter, every digraph and the tonos.

## The six units

| # | Unit | Focus | Skills |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Τα εικοσιτέσσερα γράμματα | The twenty-four letters: names, capital and lower-case forms, the two sigmas (σ / ς) | reading, language_awareness |
| 2 | Ήχοι και γράμματα | Sound values letter by letter; γ, χ and κ before front and back vowels; the letters that all sound /i/ | listening, language_awareness |
| 3 | Δίψηφα φωνήεντα | Vowel digraphs: αι, ει, οι, ου, and the αυ / ευ pair with its voiced and voiceless readings | reading, listening |
| 4 | Δίψηφα σύμφωνα | Consonant digraphs and clusters: μπ, ντ, γκ, γγ, τσ, τζ — word-initially and word-internally | reading, listening |
| 5 | Ο τόνος και τα διαλυτικά | Monotonic stress: where the tonos goes, why it moves, what the diaeresis does (παϊδάκια, ρολόι) | writing, language_awareness |
| 6 | Διαβάζω ελληνικά | Reading fluency: signs, menus, timetables and names; typing monotonic Greek on a keyboard | reading, writing |

Each unit follows the standard five-step shape — Activate, Input, Practise,
Apply, Reflect — with an answer key, a teacher note, a slide deck, a worksheet
and audio.

{{< callout type="note" title="Transliteration policy" >}}
Latin transliteration (ELOT 743) is used as a **scaffold inside Level 0 and for
glossary headwords only**. It disappears at the end of Unit 6 and does not
reappear in A1, A2 or B1. The point of Level 0 is that the learner reads Greek,
not Latinised Greek.
{{< /callout >}}

## Sound and spelling are not symmetrical

Greek spelling→sound is largely predictable: a letter sequence has one reading,
and Level 0 teaches those readings exhaustively. Sound→spelling is not:
**ι, η, υ, ει and οι all represent /i/**, and **ο and ω** both represent /o/.
No amount of listening resolves this — it is learned per word, and it is a
spelling problem, not a pronunciation problem.

Level 0 says so plainly, separates the two directions, and hands the
sound→spelling problem to the vocabulary work of A1 onward, where it belongs.
The two CEFR linguistic-competence scales this level directly serves are
`LING.orthographic-control` and `LING.phonological-control`.

## What "done" looks like

A learner leaving Level 0 can:

- name and write all twenty-four letters, upper and lower case, and use final
  sigma correctly;
- read aloud an unfamiliar Greek word — including one containing digraphs — and
  be understood;
- place the tonos correctly when copying a written word, and recognise when a
  diaeresis is doing real work;
- type monotonic Greek, with accents, on a standard keyboard layout.
